From Individual to Collaborative Extended Reality: Workspace-Aware Navigation, Data Sense-Making, and Real-World Applications

Abstract

Extended Reality (XR) has matured from an experimental laboratory apparatus into consumer-ready tools for everyday interactions. Yet it has been developed and evaluated mainly for individual users, even for tasks that are inherently collaborative. This thesis examines what changes when XR is designed for collaboration from the outset, along three axes: how users navigate within constrained physical spaces, how they make sense of data together, and how these capabilities translate to professional and educational practice.

The first chapter addresses navigation through a systematic review of workspace-aware locomotion and two case studies on workspace-aware teleportation and point-of-interest-based portals for museum visits. The second reviews a decade of collaborative immersive analytics and presents applications involving honey bee and meteorological data. The third examines XR in open liver surgery, archaeology and cultural heritage, and soft-skills training.

Across these contributions, collaboration changes the problem more than simply scaling it up: users’ physical and virtual workspaces, roles, and devices may diverge, yet must remain coherent. The manuscript concludes with design lessons, limitations, and perspectives on multi-user, asynchronous, and ecologically valid collaborative extended reality.
